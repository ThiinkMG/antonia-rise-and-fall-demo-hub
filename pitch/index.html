I · the game in one minute

What is Antonia?

Fight for every wall. Then make the bargain that keeps it.

The game

A free-to-play strategy game for phones. Roam the land on foot or horseback, grow a city, raise an army and take kingdoms in short, hands-on siege battles. Thousands of other players share your world as allies or rivals.

Who you are

You grew up on a farm in the hills above Lysa, loved, and knowing nothing of where you came from. Then one day you found a note that was never meant to be found by any hand but yours. You never speak a word in the story. What you do speaks for you.

The stakes

The Remian Empire forces city after city to kneel to one chained god. But worship taken by fear burns bright and fades, so the empire must keep taking. Only loyalty given freely holds the world together.

What's different

Sieges are quick battles of skill, and every wall you break strengthens your hand at the bargaining table. Broken promises scar the map. Money buys style, never power.

III · the story so far

Told by your mother

Your mother, Persephi, is sealed away where no sun has ever risen. She sends you the pages of a book that writes itself, one at a time, up through the wells of Antonia.

The Book of Providence shows what may happen, never what must. The first pages are here: the note that started everything, your mother's first words to you and how the gods made the world. The rest wait in the game.

The pages are still writing. The story of Antonia can change.

IV · the world

Who rules whom

Everything in Antonia runs on a chain of devotion. Mortals worship the gods. The gods must worship Toaa, “The One Above All,” who was before all things and whose true name unmakes anyone who tries to speak it.

Devotion flows up the chain like rivers to the sea. When the god Aerickleus made the city of Remia worship him alone, the chain broke and the world began to fray.

And not all devotion is equal. A prayer given freely holds, like rope. A prayer forced out of frightened people burns bright, then frays like thread.

The game works the same way. Tribute, loyalty and oaths are how power moves, from your vassals all the way up to the gods.

The three realms

The world of Antonia is stacked in three layers. Gods above, mortals in the middle, the forgotten below.

Orthys

above · realm of the gods

White terraces above the clouds, home of Cassius, Hemera and the rest of the divine family, each ruling a piece of the world such as the sea, the harvest, love, war or death. The gods live on mortal worship and owe their own to Toaa. Since the chain broke, Orthys has gone hungry.

Antonia

the middle · where you play

The mortal continent: city-states, farms, temples and legions, reshaped by the Wheel each season. This is the land you roam, build on and fight over.

Remia

Once an ordinary city. Now the capital of the empire and the center of every map.

Lysa

A warm sandstone city under a Remian garrison, below the hills where you grew up. Your first siege, and maybe your first home again.

The Concord's ruins

Old meeting grounds where the cities once swore peace on Oath Stones. The Oathwardens still guard them.

The Hollow

below · the underworld

The dark beneath the world. It does not burn; it forgets. Colours fade, names slip, echoes come back wrong. Gods that mortals stopped worshipping sink here, along with souls nobody mourned, and they are hungry to be remembered.

The Hollow Palace

Persephi's prison, sealed so no god or mortal can reach her.

The Pit

The deepest part of the Hollow, where Aerickleus is chained for all eternity. His voice still gets out.

Empires and powers

Three kinds of power share the continent. You will start as the smallest of them.

the enemy

The Remian Empire

The largest power in Antonia. It began when the god Aerickleus made the city of Remia worship him alone. Today his old friend Corvus rules as emperor in his name, and Aerickleus speaks through him from the Pit.

What it wants: every city kneeling, until Aerickleus is strong enough to break his chains. Inside, it argues with itself: the Throne, the Choir and a Senate that is quietly tired of war.

In the game: Remian garrisons hold cities across the map. Freeing them, and finally Remia itself, is the goal of Book One.

the old order

The Concord of Many Altars

The golden age before the empire. Free city-states honored every god in turn and kept the peace with oaths sworn on sacred clay. It fell apart when Remia broke away.

What is left: the Oathwardens, neutral judges who still record every pact and mark anyone who breaks one.

In the game: the Concord is why oaths, claims and treaties carry real weight.

the independents

The Free Leagues

Cities and clans the empire has not swallowed yet: Kallos the merchant republic, Thera the oracle city, the hill clans of Doros and Mira on the sea. Each follows its own god and has its own temper.

What they want: to stay free, of the empire and of you.

In the game: these are the kingdoms you bargain with, besiege, rule as vassals or win over as allies.

Faiths

Worship is power in Antonia, so religion is never only about belief. Who a city prays to decides who grows stronger.

the old faith

The Many Altars

Worship of the whole family of gods, a temple for each. Mortals pray to the gods, and the gods pass a share of that devotion up to Toaa. This is the faith that keeps the chain whole.

In the game: pick a patron god each season for blessings, and build temples to keep your people content.

the empire's faith

The Aerinant Remisan Orthodoxy

One god only: Aerickleus. Its followers are called Reminants. Its priests, the Choir of the Tongue, carry his voice from city to city and make conquered people kneel. Worship taken by force burns bright at first and then fades.

In the game: defeat a Chorister and the empire's hold on that region weakens.

the forbidden faith

The Ashen Choir

Mortals who pray to the forgotten gods of the Hollow, hoping to raise them again. They gather wherever the land has been stripped bare.

In the game: overharvest a shared granary and their raiders appear at your gates.

And above every faith: Toaa. Mortals build it no temples. The gods themselves are its worshippers.

V · gameplay you can try right here

Try it yourself

Strategy built on game theory, not timers.

Most of your time in Antonia goes into four things: taking cities, keeping promises, sharing land with your neighbors and running your kingdom while you're away. Each is built on a real idea from game theory, the study of how people decide when their choices affect each other. The four games below are small, playable versions. You can win or lose every one of them.

These were the first idea tests. The game has grown since: sieges are now short, hands-on battles, and the Steward is becoming a defence you lay out yourself. Newer demos, like the three-minute Siege of Lysa battle, live in the Antonia Demo Hub.

game one · the siege clock

Every hour of war costs both sides

You are camped outside the city of Lysa. You want tribute, and they want you gone. Every six-hour watch drains your supplies and their morale, so each rejected offer costs you both. Squeeze too hard and you starve first.

Built on the war of attrition and Rubinstein bargaining.

VI · empire

Rise. Rule. Fall.

Conquest is the start of a relationship, and every empire is one bad ruler away from collapse.

Siege

Play with others. Fight them only if you choose.

Each season brings a newly shaped map full of computer-controlled kingdoms with their own gods, memories and moods.

  • Safe rings. Villages and small kingdoms to conquer. Other players can't attack you here.

IX · fair by design

Rule wider, not harder

Spend on style. Never on power.

Risks and challenges

Antonia is at the early prototype stage. The world and story are written and the first small demos are playable, but there is no full game yet. Here is what could go wrong and how we plan for it.

  • ScopeAn online strategy game is a large project. We will build one loop at a time, starting with the siege battle and the vassal loop, and cut features before we cut quality.
  • TechnologyServers, shared maps and fair play between players are hard to build well. Book One starts with seeded maps and computer kingdoms. Player-versus-player stays opt-in and comes after the core works.
  • ScheduleGame schedules slip. We will share milestones as they are reached and will not promise a launch date before there is a prototype to show.
  • FundingA campaign may cover only part of development. The campaign page will say plainly what each funding level pays for.
  • Fair play costs moneyRefusing to sell power means earning less than pay-to-win games. We accept that. It is the reason this game exists.

Player research

What players say about today's kingdom-building phone games, and how Antonia answers.

The short version

Players of today's biggest kingdom-building phone games love the alliances and the friends they make. Almost everything else frustrates them.

  • Four complaints come up again and again: ads that show a different game, pressure to pay, daily chores, and support they don't trust.
  • The games still earn a lot of money, so their makers have little reason to change. That gap is Antonia's opening.
  • Antonia answers each complaint with a design rule built into the game, not a promise.

each problem and its answer

What players sayWhat Antonia does

We looked most closely at Kingshot, one of the top-earning games of this kind, and at its nearest rivals.

The ads sell a different game

The most common complaint about Kingshot is that its ads show a tower-defense game, while the real game is about building a kingdom and joining alliance wars.

what players say

  • Reviewers say they downloaded it for tower defense and found that part was a small side activity. One App Store reviewer called it "elementary at best."
  • A Google Play review calling the ads misleading had been marked helpful by more than 2,700 people.
  • One review-analysis site rates the overall mood as "Frustrated" across about 1.4 million ratings and names false advertising as the top concern.
  • Industry analysts note that tower defense is a small share of the game and mostly serves the advertising.

insight

Players aren't angry at what the game is. They're angry that they were promised something else.

Antonia's answer:

You have to pay to keep up

Players say that competing, especially inside an alliance, becomes close to impossible without buying packs.

what players say

  • A review roundup reports that players find it nearly impossible to compete in alliances without premium packs.
  • One long review says that once big spenders settle on a server, everyone else ends up serving them or avoiding them. It describes ordinary members as playing "attendance software with swords."
  • An App Store reviewer said they are happy to pay for cosmetics or to remove ads, but felt this game asks you to pay just to compete.
  • A 2026 review says the late game feels aggressive about money if you want to compete seriously.

the other side

Defenders point out that nothing in the game is locked behind a purchase. Money buys speed. The complaint is about how much that speed decides.

insight

Spending isn't the problem. The problem is that spending decides fights between players.

Antonia's answer:

It feels like a second job

Daily checklists, timers and events at fixed hours make players feel they have to show up, whether or not they're having fun.

what players say

  • One Google Play reviewer wrote that it "feels more like a second job, not a fun game."
  • Reviews describe constant check-ins and recurring events that punish you for missing them.
  • A 2026 review recommends the game only to people ready to treat it as a long-term hobby, and tells casual players to skip it.

insight

Players who log in out of fear of falling behind are not enjoying themselves. Sooner or later they quit.

Antonia's answer:

Support isn't trusted

Some players believe big spenders get better treatment, and that leaving the game is made harder than it should be.

what players say

  • The publisher scores 2.0 out of 5 on Trustpilot. That is from only 11 reviews, so treat it as a signal, not a verdict.
  • One of those reviewers claims that reports about bad behavior are handled according to a player's VIP level.
  • A review-analysis site lists account deletion as a complaint, with users calling the process "intentionally convoluted."

These are individual claims that we cannot verify. They matter because they show what players have come to expect.

insight

Fair treatment has to be visible. If players can't see that the rules apply to everyone, they assume the worst.

Antonia's answer:

Why players stay anyway

People stay for other people. The alliances, rivalries and friendships are what these games get right.

what players say

  • One App Store reviewer said the only reason they still play is the friends they have made from around the world, even though they wish they had never downloaded the game.
  • Reviewers praise the diplomacy, the timing of group attacks and the politics between alliances as stories no single-player game could script.
  • Players also like that the game doesn't force them to watch ads.

insight

The social side is the product. A better game keeps it at the center and removes what poisons it.

Advertise the real game

Antonia's rule: nothing appears in an ad that a new player can't do in their first hour.

how it works

  • The exciting parts are the main game. Sieges, bargaining against the clock and broken oaths are what you do every day, not a side mode.
  • Those moments look good in a short clip: a rope burning between two cities, an oath tether snapping, a valley turning brown.
  • You can try small versions of them on this page before you ever download the game.

insight

Honest ads bring in players who want the game we actually made, and those players stay.

Answers:

Sell style, never power

Antonia's rule: money changes how your kingdom looks, never what it can do. A free player can always match a paying one.

how it works

  • Every product must pass one check: it changes looks, not power.
  • For sale: skins for your hero, troops and mounts, banners, monuments and city decorations, and a seasonal Book Pass with a free track.
  • Never for sale: troop power, mounts or companions, extra vassals or trade routes, speed-ups, or random loot boxes.
  • We will track how often big spenders beat skilled free players and tighten the rules if the gap grows.

insight

Winning comes from bargaining, scouting and keeping your word. None of those can be bought.

Answers:

Respect the player's time

Antonia's rule: the game should fit around your life, not the other way round.

how it works

  • Siege battles last about three minutes. Your walls, towers and garrisons defend you while you are offline, so a night's sleep doesn't cost you your kingdom.
  • Diplomatic offers wait 8 to 12 hours for an answer. Nobody needs a 3 a.m. alarm.
  • The daily list is three meaningful tasks, not twenty chores.
  • Side activities can be skipped for most of the reward.

insight

Players who can step away without being punished come back because they want to.

Answers:

Keep the alliances, lose the bullying

Antonia's rule: playing with other people is the heart of the game. Being preyed on by them is a choice you opt into.

how it works

  • Most of the map is safe. You conquer computer-controlled kingdoms, and other players can't attack you there.
  • Fighting other players happens only in the heartland, a zone you choose to enter for bigger rewards.
  • Alliances still matter everywhere: shared sieges, trade, pacts and a server-wide goal.
  • Reports are judged on what was said or done, never on how much someone has spent. Deleting your account is simple.

insight

The friendships are why people stay. Protecting new and casual players protects the friendships.
